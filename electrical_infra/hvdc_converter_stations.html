HVDC Converter Stations

Role and Cost Boundary

An offshore HVDC converter station changes the wind farm’s collected AC power to DC for export. A second station onshore changes it back to grid-compatible AC. This page owns the converter electrical equipment at both terminals, but not the offshore support structure or its installation.

Reference Configuration

The electrical design reference is TenneT’s 2 GW North Sea standard (TenneT 2024, 2025):

Parameter Reference value
Rated active-power transfer 2 GW
Offshore collection voltage 66 kV AC
Converter technology VSC using modular multilevel converters
HVDC configuration Bipole with dedicated metallic return
Pole voltage +/-525 kV
Offshore generation blocks 4 x 500 MW
Offshore HVDC transformers Four
Onshore grid voltage 380 kV AC
Normal full-load pole current 1.905 kA
Healthy-pole degraded transfer Approximately 1 GW

For balanced bipolar operation:

\[ P_\mathrm{dc}=2V_\mathrm{pole}I_\mathrm{pole}. \]

At 2 GW and 525 kV per pole, \(I_\mathrm{pole}=1.905\) kA. The converter MVA rating must additionally cover reactive-power duty, voltage range, harmonics, overloads and grid-code margins.

Converter Cost Model

DEA Basis

The Danish Energy Agency gives a 2025 central planning value of 320 EUR/kW for an onshore 525 kV DC converter station, with 250–420 EUR/kW as its low and high cases. These values are stated at the 2020 price level (Danish Energy Agency 2025).

The report’s factor of 1.263 restates 2020 values through forecast 2026 inflation. For the common EUR2025 basis, the 3.0% forecast 2026 step is removed explicitly:

\[ F_{2020\rightarrow2025} = \frac{1.263}{1.030} = 1.22621. \]

The transformation uses euro-area annual-average HICP through 2025 and the European Commission’s 3.0% euro-area inflation forecast for 2026 (Eurostat 2026; European Commission 2026).

Because the DEA offshore-station value explicitly includes its platform, it is not used as converter equipment CAPEX here. The model provisionally applies the onshore-station coefficient to both electrical terminals:

Converter electrical equipment Low Central High Cost owner
One converter station 307 EUR2025/kW 392 EUR2025/kW 515 EUR2025/kW This page
Offshore + onshore converter pair 613 EUR2025/kW 785 EUR2025/kW 1,030 EUR2025/kW This page

The adopted central coefficient is 785 EUR2025/kW for the two-terminal converter pair. No residual project factor is added. Owner development, financing, wider grid works and decommissioning remain outside this coefficient.

The offshore terminal may require more costly marinised electrical equipment than the onshore terminal. A public offshore-equipment premium separated from platform and installation cost is not currently available, so this is a stated modelling limitation.

The grey values show the complete physical context. Their derivation, uncertainty and booking rules are maintained on the linked pages. Only the green converter coefficient is booked here.

Public TenneT programme announcements correspond to roughly EUR 1,000–1,170/kW when divided by announced system count and rating (TenneT 2023; Hitachi Energy and Petrofac 2023; GE Vernova 2023; Siemens Energy 2023). These are broad infrastructure-package comparisons, not converter-only prices. Their scopes are more comparable with the DEA offshore-plus-onshore station range, which includes the platform, than with the 785 EUR2025/kW converter-only coefficient.

Conversion Losses

Converter-station losses include semiconductor conduction and switching, transformer copper and core loss, reactors, cooling and station auxiliaries. Supplier loss curves are not yet available, so the model uses aggregate working efficiencies:

Boundary Working efficiency Interpretation
Offshore conversion, transformation and auxiliaries 98.5% Applied once offshore
Onshore conversion, transformation and auxiliaries 98.5% Applied once onshore

Hitachi Energy reports electrical losses below 1% per converter station for DolWin2 (Hitachi Energy, n.d.). This is an OEM statement for a 900 MW, +/-320 kV symmetrical-monopole system commissioned in 2016; it supports the order of magnitude but is not a guarantee for a 2 GW, +/-525 kV station.

Together, the two 98.5% terminal values give a working station-pair efficiency of 97.0%. The values include transformers and auxiliaries; no separate substation or transformer factor is applied to the same equipment.

Availability and Degraded Operation

The legacy model applies 98% availability to each terminal. These are internal energy-equivalent factors, not supplier guarantees. A more physical model distinguishes:

State Available export capacity Example cause
Both poles and terminals healthy 2 GW Normal operation
One pole path unavailable Approximately 1 GW Pole converter, transformer or cable outage
Both poles unavailable 0 GW Common control, auxiliary or platform event

Capacity-weighted availability is:

\[ A_E = 1- \frac{\sum_i\Delta P_i\Delta t_i} {P_\mathrm{rated}T}. \]

Once these states are simulated, the corresponding flat availability factors must be removed.

Model Limitations

Limitation Implication
Offshore converter uses the onshore cost coefficient Offshore marinisation and logistics premium are not represented in converter CAPEX
Platform and installation costs remain incompletely separated The complete offshore-station cost is not yet a fully additive bottom-up estimate
HICP is a general price index HVDC equipment escalation may differ from consumer inflation
Linear EUR/kW scaling Discrete equipment and platform thresholds are hidden
Constant station efficiencies Part-load, no-load and temperature effects are hidden
Flat terminal availabilities Pole redundancy and common-mode failures are simplified
