Hydrogen Infield Infrastructure

Role and Scope

Hydrogen infield infrastructure collects compressed hydrogen from turbine-integrated electrolysers and delivers it to the export-pipeline connection. It applies only to the decentralised hydrogen architecture. The electricity-export and centralised-hydrogen architectures use 66 kV AC infield cables.

This page defines the proposed network topology and the analytical pipeline-length model. Pipeline material, pressure and capacity are covered on Hydrogen Pipelines. Installation is covered on Cable and Pipeline Installation.

Proposed Ladder Layout

The proposed network is a ladder. Two longitudinal header pipelines run along opposite sides of the turbine field. Cross-farm pipelines form the rungs. Turbines connect directly to the rung that passes through their row.

The layout is used for four reasons:

  • A 6 inch composite pipeline at 150 bar is assumed to carry about 500 MW of hydrogen on an HHV-equivalent basis. One rung can therefore collect a complete turbine row without the 60–70 MW string limit of a 66 kV AC cable (Project Team 2026).
  • Each turbine can send hydrogen to either header. Isolation of one local rung or header segment does not automatically isolate every turbine behind that segment.
  • The regular grid follows the turbine rows and avoids a separate radial route from every turbine to a central platform.
  • Flexible composite pipe can be supplied in long reeled lengths. This suits repeated infield runs and reduces the number of offshore field joints relative to rigid steel pipe (Project Team 2026).

The topology provides two infield paths, not two independent export systems. Both paths ultimately reach the same export-pipeline interface. Valves, isolation philosophy and the capacity of the remaining path determine whether the redundancy can be used after a fault.

Ladder Geometry for the Reference Case

The geometry follows the common coordinates of the agreed reference case: a rectangle of breadth \(B\) across the dominant wind and length \(L\) along it, with \(n_L\) crosswind rows of up to \(n_B\) turbines. Turbine centres span the rectangle, and the shorter final row is centred.

  • Rungs follow the crosswind rows. Each rung spans the full breadth between the two headers, so its horizontal length is \(B\).

  • Headers run along-wind through the ends of the rows. Each spans from the first to the last row, so its horizontal length is \(L\).

  • Turbine connections: every turbine sits on its row’s rung. The rung is divided into sections between consecutive turbines; in full rows the end turbines lie on the headers, while the shorter final row needs one extension section at each end to reach them.

  • Tie-ins join the midpoint of each header to the export manifold at the farm centre, running crosswind along the centreline between the two middle rows; each is \(B/2\) long.

The horizontal ladder length, in km, is:

\[ L_\mathrm{ladder}=n_L B+2L+B. \]

Orienting the rungs along the crosswind rows gives fewer, longer rungs and shorter headers than rungs along the columns. The earlier square-farm estimate (\(\sqrt{A}(\sqrt{N}+2)\) for 200 km² and 134 turbines, 192–198 km) is superseded for the article case.

Manifold Boundary and Tie-ins

The export manifold is at the centre of the farm (agreed 25 September 2026). It is the boundary between infield collection and the export pipeline: sections upstream of it belong on this page, and the export route length is measured from it. Each header drains to its midpoint, so both header halves carry at most half of their side’s rows, and a tie-in from each header midpoint carries that header’s flow to the manifold. Figure 1 shows the ladder topology schematically; it places the export interface at an edge and does not show this central tie-in routing.

Model Representation and Healthy-State Flow

The model reads the collection network as explicit sections (see the model guide): each section has its end nodes, vertical length, route allowance, healthy-state flow share, true internal diameter and absolute inlet and outlet pressures. Non-turbine nodes, such as header nodes and the manifold, are listed separately. Every physical section is included, even if its healthy-state flow is zero.

The healthy-state allocation used for the reference case is:

  • each rung splits at its centre, with half the row flowing to each header; in a row with an odd turbine count, the middle turbine sends half its flow each way;
  • each header flows from its ends toward its midpoint;
  • each tie-in carries one header’s flow, half the farm, to the manifold.

All sections carry flow in this healthy state. At rated output, a full row delivers 127.5 MW to each header, the most heavily loaded header section carries 510 MW and each tie-in 1005 MW (electrical input equivalent; hydrogen flow follows from the stack model).

prepare_common_case.py writes the network nodes (common/collection_nodes.csv) and the section geometry with these flow shares (common/collection_sections_geometry.csv). Vertical length is one water depth per turbine end; header nodes and the manifold are on the seabed, and no route allowance is applied. Section diameters and pressures are design inputs of each case, joined to this geometry in the O7 pressure–diameter study; they are not fixed by the geometry.

Scope of the Length Result

The calculated length includes the horizontal rungs and the two horizontal headers. It excludes:

  • the export pipeline from the manifold to shore;
  • turbine risers, local tie-ins and short spool pieces;
  • the export manifold equipment itself;
  • route deviations around seabed constraints, crossings and exclusion zones;
  • installation slack and contingency; and
  • parallel pipe added where segment capacity is insufficient.

No 20% routing allowance is included in the current hydrogen base case. The source applies that allowance to the analytical AC-cable layout, not to the hydrogen ladder. A separate routing factor may be applied before detailed routing:

\[ L_\mathrm{routed} = L_\mathrm{infield} \left(1+f_\mathrm{route}\right). \]

Do not apply this factor to a GIS-derived route that already includes deviations and slack.

Flow and Pressure Checks Versus Redundancy

Current checks (healthy state). For the supplied sections and flow shares, the model conserves flow at every node, checks that each section’s capacity covers its peak flow at the supplied inlet and outlet pressures, and rejects a pressure rise at a junction without a compressor. The legacy claim that one 6 inch rung at 150 bar carries about 500 MW supports a single row (127.5 MW per row end) and the most loaded header section (510 MW). It does not cover the tie-ins at 1005 MW each, which need a larger diameter, parallel pipe or a higher pressure; that choice belongs to the pressure–diameter study.

Deferred (D3). Fault states, isolation valves, rerouting through the second header and spare capacity are not analysed. The flow allocation above is not solved as a hydraulic loop network, and the model claims no redundancy benefit. The 100% infield-pipeline availability is a modelling convention, not a reliability result.
