Power Electronics

Role in the Value Chain

Electrolyser power electronics convert the 66 kV infield AC supply to the controlled low-voltage DC required by the PEM stacks.

This page represents the complete conversion chain between the 66 kV platform input and the stack DC terminals as one aggregated subsystem. It includes the plant step-down transformer, rectifier transformers, rectifier cabinets, converter controls, and dedicated conversion-system cooling. Turbine-side conversion equipment is covered in Generator and Power Electronics. The PEM stack and its polarisation behaviour are covered in Stack. Hydrogen compression is covered separately in Compressor.

The main 66 kV transformer is included on this page and should not be costed again in another electrical-infrastructure component. Harmonic filtering, phase shifting, reactive-power management, and converter cooling are not represented as separate systems; their normal provision is included within the aggregated package cost and net efficiency.

Reference Architecture

The reference configuration is a water-cooled, 24-pulse thyristor rectifier supplied through a phase-shifting rectifier transformer. Multi-pulse thyristor conversion remains a common industrial solution at multi-megawatt scale because it combines high efficiency, low semiconductor cost, high current capability, and established reliability. Contemporary commercial systems are offered in 6-, 12-, 18-, and 24-pulse arrangements, with harmonic distortion decreasing as pulse number increases (ABB 2025). Reviews of large-scale electrolyser converters likewise identify thyristor rectifiers as the established high-power option, while active-front-end and chopper-based systems trade higher cost and conversion complexity for improved power quality and controllability (Yodwong et al. 2020; Chen et al. 2022).

The 66 kV input is represented as an aggregated two-stage voltage reduction:

\[ 66\ \mathrm{kV\ AC} \rightarrow \mathrm{medium\mbox{-}voltage\ AC} \rightarrow \mathrm{controlled\ stack\ DC}. \]

The intermediate voltage and detailed transformer winding arrangement are not modelled. Large-plant studies use comparable high-voltage-to-medium-voltage distribution followed by repeated transformer–rectifier modules, including PEM layouts based on approximately 40 MW power blocks (Noordende and Ripson 2020).

Cost Basis

Published power-electronics costs are not fully like-for-like. The literature includes manufactured cost, purchased-equipment cost, and installed system cost, with different transformer and auxiliary-equipment boundaries. Where a source explicitly reports manufactured cost before markup, the same 50% manufacturer markup used for the stack and balance-of-plant costs is applied here before comparison (see Financial Assumptions), rather than treating any single source as a directly transferable quotation.

NREL adopts a cost of 199 USD/kW for the transformer and rectifier of a 1 MW PEM electrolyser, based on historical supplier quotations and explicitly excluding manufacturer markup, installation, soft costs, and inflation (Badgett et al. 2024). Applying the standard 50% markup gives an equipment purchase cost of:

\[ 1.5 \times 199 \approx 300 \ \mathrm{USD_{2020}/kW} \]

Fraunhofer ISE estimates PEM power-electronics costs of approximately 160 EUR/kW in its 2020 cases and approximately 101–102 EUR/kW in its 2030 cases, reported at the system/investment level rather than as a bare manufactured cost. Its 100 MW cases add a separate high-voltage transformer of approximately 25–26 EUR/kW, giving a combined large-system estimate of approximately 185–186 EUR/kW. The specific cost of the power electronics changes only marginally between the 5 MW and 100 MW cases (Holst et al. 2021). A recent industrial comparison reports substantially lower Chinese-market investment costs for 5 MW packages: approximately CNY 270/kW for a thyristor rectifier including its phase-shifting transformer, compared with CNY 320/kW for a diode-plus-chopper system and CNY 400/kW for an IGBT system (Gao et al. 2025). No CNY-to-EUR conversion is applied here: the comparison is used only to confirm that thyristor rectifiers are the lower-cost topology, not as a quantitative anchor, because the geography, project scope, installation boundary, and procurement conditions differ too much for a direct rate conversion to be meaningful.

No public vendor source was found that provides a binding price for a complete offshore 66 kV-to-DC package with a consistent scope. Vendor literature is therefore used to confirm topology, modularity, and efficiency, but not as the primary cost basis.

The central model value is 185 EUR/kW for the complete purchased 66 kV-to-stack-DC conversion package, taken directly from Fraunhofer’s combined large-system transformer-plus-power-electronics estimate. This sits well below the marked-up NREL benchmark of approximately 300 USD/kW for a small 1 MW reference unit — compared here at face value, pending a documented USD-to-EUR conversion factor (see Financial Assumptions) — and above the lowest reported regional equipment prices.

Cost Scaling

No six-tenths-rule scaling is applied. The subsystem is treated as repeated transformer–rectifier power blocks, giving a scaling exponent of 1.00.

This assumption is based on practical unit limits rather than an assumption that electrical equipment has no economies of scale. Industrial rectifiers operate at very high current and are built from parallel semiconductor devices and repeated bridges because a single device cannot supply the complete plant current (Gao et al. 2025). Commercial high-power converter units are typically limited to the multi-megawatt range, after which plant capacity is increased mainly by adding modules (Chen et al. 2022). Large electrolyser designs similarly number up repeated stack and transformer–rectifier blocks rather than enlarging one converter indefinitely (Noordende and Ripson 2020).

Fraunhofer’s bottom-up results support this approximation: specific power-electronics cost is nearly constant between 5 MW and 100 MW, unlike centralised process equipment that benefits more strongly from shared capacity (Holst et al. 2021). Some plant-level economies can still arise from shared engineering, controls, switchgear, buildings, and procurement. The exponent of 1.00 should therefore be interpreted as a conservative purchased-equipment assumption, not proof that total installed electrical-system cost is perfectly linear.

Energy Balance

For centralised electrolysis, the model applies one net conversion loss between the 66 kV AC input and the stack DC terminals:

\[ P_{\mathrm{stack,DC}} = P_{\mathrm{in,66kV}} \left(1-L_{\mathrm{conversion}}\right). \]

Net power-conversion assumption for centralised electrolysis.
Parameter Central assumption Sensitivity range
Net 66 kV AC-to-stack-DC efficiency 97.5% 96.0–98.5%
Net conversion loss 2.5% 1.5–4.0%

The 2.5% loss includes the plant step-down transformer, rectifier transformers, rectifier semiconductor losses, and dedicated conversion-system cooling. It does not include infield cable losses, general platform auxiliaries, or stack electrochemical losses.

The value represents a modern, high-efficiency thyristor package rather than a generic historical fleet average. ABB reports rectifier-only efficiency of up to 99.6% for its current water-cooled thyristor product (ABB 2025). An industrial comparison reports thyristor rectifier efficiency above approximately 98.5% and complete transformer-plus-rectifier efficiency above 97.5% (Gao et al. 2025). Fraunhofer assumes rectifier efficiencies of 96% for its 2020 design and 98% for its 2030 design, with a separate high-voltage transformer efficiency of 99% (Holst et al. 2021). The selected 97.5% net value is therefore representative of an efficient contemporary package while retaining losses for the full 66 kV-to-DC chain.

A constant net loss is used at all plant loads. In practice, efficiency depends on loading, transformer no-load losses, firing angle, cooling demand, and current ripple. Multi-pulse conversion and modular dispatch reduce, but do not eliminate, these effects. The loss sensitivity should be used where the annual operating profile contains substantial low-load operation.

Availability

Transformer and rectifier outages are included in the overall electrolyser availability factor and are not applied as an additional derating on this page.

The physical system is expected to consist of multiple conversion blocks, so failure of one block may cause partial rather than complete plant unavailability. Explicit block redundancy, spare strategy, failure rates, and offshore repair logistics are outside the current model resolution.

Model Limitations

This model is intended for early-stage techno-economic assessment. Several previous limitations are resolved by defining a complete 66 kV-to-DC boundary, selecting a reference converter topology, and using a net conversion efficiency. The remaining limitations are:

Remaining limitations of the power-electronics model.
Limitation Implication
The 185 EUR/kW value is triangulated from literature rather than obtained from a project-specific vendor quotation The value should be replaced by an RFQ when the stack voltage range, block rating, redundancy, offshore environmental class, and delivery scope are known
Source costs use different years, markets, and commercial boundaries; the marked-up NREL and Fraunhofer figures are compared at face value across currencies without a documented USD-to-EUR conversion factor The central value is less precise than a like-for-like competitive tender and should be tested over the stated 100–300 EUR/kW range
Linear scaling represents numbering up of modular power blocks Shared plant engineering and high-voltage infrastructure may produce some installed-cost economies that are not captured
A 24-pulse thyristor rectifier is used as the reference topology Projects selecting an IGBT active-front-end or diode-plus-chopper topology may have different CAPEX, efficiency, controllability, and power-quality performance
A fixed 2.5% net loss is used for every operating point Load-dependent transformer, rectifier, cooling, and ripple losses are not resolved; the 1.5–4.0% sensitivity should be used for uncertain operating profiles
Offshore adaptation and redundancy are included only through the cost range Marinisation, corrosion protection, enclosed cooling, fire protection, and N+1 requirements are not separately designed or costed
Availability is bundled into the overall electrolyser factor Component-level outages, partial block availability, and offshore repair time are not represented explicitly
The main 66 kV transformer is assigned to this subsystem Model integration must ensure that the same transformer is not also included in platform or infield electrical CAPEX
