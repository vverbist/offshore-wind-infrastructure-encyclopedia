Electricity and Hydrogen: Comparing Energy Carriers for Offshore Wind, over the entire value chain

The premise: two carriers, each given its own best system

This research compares two energy carriers for offshore wind — electricity and hydrogen — by designing a complete energy system around each and placing the results side by side. The carrier is the one choice fixed in advance, deliberately, because it is the variable under study. Everything downstream of that choice is left free: generation, collection, conversion, transport, storage, and delivery are designed as one coupled problem, and each carrier gets the architecture that suits it best rather than the architecture built for the other.

Two things distinguish this from most existing techno-economic work on offshore wind and its carrier options. First, scope of the design variables. Many studies comparing transport methods or conversion routes hold turbine spacing, power density, and layout fixed as inputs. Here they are outputs of the same design problem as everything else, because they are physically coupled to it: export capacity depends on how power is aggregated, which depends on where conversion happens, which depends on spacing, which sets the wake losses that shape the production profile the rest of the chain has to handle. Fixing any one of these while designing the rest quietly fixes an assumption the comparison should instead be free to test. Second, scope of the chain itself. The comparison runs through the full chain to delivery, not to the offshore/onshore interface. Where the demand being served is electrical, reconversion equipment and losses belong on the ledger exactly as much as export cables do. Nothing is compared at a midpoint that happens to flatter one option over the other. What that demand consists of — its composition, location, and timing — is not fixed by the research but specified per scenario, so that the comparison can be drawn for different end-uses on the same transparent terms.

The comparison is deliberately between pure systems. Hybrid configurations, in which both carriers leave the farm side by side, are excluded by design — not because they are implausible, but because the question here is comparative rather than global. The aim is not to find the single best North Sea system; it is to isolate what each carrier’s cost structure does to the optimal design when the system is organized entirely around it. A hybrid would blur exactly the contrast the research is built to expose, and the two pure systems bound the space in which any hybrid would sit.

Why efficiency is not the objective

This is not a new problem for engineers. A plant built from locally efficient unit operations is not guaranteed to be the cheapest plant to build and run, which is precisely why process design happens at the flowsheet level, not unit by unit. Offshore energy systems have the same structure, and the carrier question is where it shows up most clearly.

Converting electricity to hydrogen and back loses energy that direct electrification does not. That is the correct, uncontested thermodynamic argument, and nothing here disputes it. What is in question is whether it is the right variable to optimize, as opposed to one cost among several to be weighed. A conversion loss is a cost like any other. It can be outweighed by savings elsewhere in the chain, the same way a less efficient piece of equipment can still be the cheaper one to own if it needs less supporting infrastructure around it.

Widen the accounting to the full chain and both carriers show a side that a production-level comparison can’t capture. Electricity’s costs surface downstream, and they come from more than one place. Transmission infrastructure is priced by peak capacity (€/MW) while revenue is earned per delivered MWh. That mismatch alone pushes designs toward protecting utilization: wider spacing, lower installed density, export links sized below peak generation, curtailment by design. A second, independent driver pushes the same way. Wind production and demand rarely line up in time, and because large-scale electrical storage is expensive, the surplus generated in high-production periods has nowhere economical to go and gets curtailed rather than stored. How large that mismatch is depends on the demand profile the system serves; curtailment is evaluated here against exogenous demand profiles, and results are read as conditional on them, with sensitivity to their shape part of the analysis. Neither driver requires the other: capacity could be perfectly sized and curtailment would still happen on the mismatch alone, just as it would happen on the capacity mismatch even with perfectly aligned demand. Both are invisible at the farm gate, where a MWh produced and a MWh delivered are treated as the same thing. High efficiency at the wire can coexist with high infrastructure cost per delivered MWh and low spatial yield.

Hydrogen’s costs and benefits both surface downstream, not just its benefits. Conversion losses are immediate and local, exactly where the thermodynamic argument places them, and if the end-use is electrical, a second conversion loss and its equipment show up again at the far end of the chain. None of that is waived. What also shows up downstream, and what a farm-gate comparison structurally can’t see, is cheaper bulk pipeline transport, cheaper large-scale storage, and export capacity that can be added more gradually than electrical capacity typically allows. A comparison drawn at the farm gate registers hydrogen’s losses and registers none of the rest, in either direction, for either carrier.

Efficiency, in other words, is an instrumental variable, not a terminal one. What is terminal is the cost of energy delivered to the end-user and the sea area required to produce it (MWh/km²), and neither outranks the other by default. Cost is the more familiar objective, so it is worth stating plainly why space carries equal weight here rather than trailing behind it as a footnote. The North Sea is not an empty canvas: shipping lanes, fishing grounds, cables, pipelines, defence areas, and nature protection all compete for the same seabed, and the area allocated to wind development is being fixed now, in blocks, for decades. A design that trades a little energy density for a large reduction in €/MWh is a good answer to a cost-minimization problem and can be a poor one where space is the binding constraint. Which of the two binds first is itself an outcome of architecture and carrier choice, not a fixed fact about the wind resource. Rather than collapsing the two into a single number, results are presented as cost–density trade-offs per carrier, so that the balance between them stays visible instead of being decided inside the model.

Whether the most efficient design is also the cheapest and most space-effective one is not something to assume in either direction. It’s the empirical question this research is built to answer.

Research question and expected mechanism

How does the choice of energy carrier alter subsystem-level marginal cost structures, and how does this reshape the economically optimal offshore wind farm design and the achievable energy density (MWh/km²)?

The expected mechanism is this: the optimal design balances the marginal cost of expanding capacity-rated infrastructure (∂C/∂MW) against the marginal value of delivered energy (€/MWh), and the carrier determines where that balance sits. Where capacity expansion is expensive and discrete, the optimum is expected to shift toward utilization preservation at the expense of installed density; where capacity scales gradually, higher density can remain optimal. Energy density would then be an emergent outcome of system design, not a fixed property of the wind resource, and it may differ by carrier. Whether it does, and by how much, is what the comparison is built to show.

The comparison: electricity versus hydrogen as the primary carrier

The central comparison places two fully worked-out systems side by side, each designed end-to-end around its own carrier:

Electricity as primary carrier. Power is aggregated electrically, transmitted onshore via standardized high-capacity HVDC blocks, and converted downstream where needed. This is the current North Sea planning paradigm, built on centralized aggregation and bespoke offshore platforms.

Hydrogen as primary carrier. Electrolysis takes place at or near each turbine, and hydrogen, not electricity, is the medium in which energy is collected, transported, and stored. Restructuring around the carrier replaces heavy central aggregation with modular, repeatable conversion units (economies of series rather than scale), collection networks that follow pipeline rather than cable logic, and peak formation, reliability, and storage dynamics distributed across many small units instead of concentrated in one platform.

Hydrogen is worth testing not because it is more efficient — it is not — but because of what sits downstream of the conversion: bulk transport by pipeline, storage at large scale, and export capacity added gradually, all far cheaper than their electrical equivalents. The conversion loss is the price paid for that cheaper chain, which is why the version worth comparing is a farm rebuilt around hydrogen’s downstream economics, not electrolysers added to an electric one.

Each system is designed on its own terms. Neither carrier is evaluated inside an architecture built for the other, which is the comparison this research is designed to avoid.

Method

The comparison is implemented as an integrated techno-economic model of the full chain, from wind resource to delivery. Rather than searching for a single mathematical optimum, the model calculates a wide range of designs for each carrier — varying spacing, conversion siting, export sizing, storage, and the other design variables together — across scenarios for wind climate, distance to shore, institutional block sizes, demand profiles, and financing. The aim is to map where the good designs lie for each carrier and how that map shifts as conditions change, not to pin down a precise best point. Robustness is judged by whether those qualitative patterns persist across the scenario range, not by point-estimate precision.

Market dynamics and price formation are outside the scope: the framing throughout is that a given demand is met at the lowest total system cost. This excludes revenue-side effects — such as the value of selling into high-price hours — for both carriers symmetrically, and keeps the comparison a question of infrastructure cost rather than market behaviour.

The dissertation is deliberately model-first rather than papers-first: the subsystems are so tightly coupled (spacing → wakes → production profile → conversion sizing → export sizing → cost → back to spacing) that isolated component studies would require freezing exactly the variables the research treats as free. The integrated model is the engine from which the papers derive.

Why this matters now

Investment and planning decisions for offshore energy infrastructure are being made now, on multi-decade horizons, under the working assumption that architecture is close to fixed and only production-level parameters remain to be optimized. If that assumption doesn’t hold — if the coupling between spacing, conversion siting, export sizing, and carrier choice materially changes the optimal design — the cost of finding out late is high: offshore infrastructure is expensive to redo and effectively impossible to change once built. Putting the two carriers on equal footing, each with the system that suits it best, and making the comparison explicit and testable now rather than leaving it implicit, is what this research is for.
